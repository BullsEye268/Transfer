Motivation for Pursuing PhD Studies
Our future will run on chips. Every system we trust, from phones to power grids to defense platforms, rests on hardware that cannot be patched once it is fabricated. In my first year as a Project Officer at Temasek Laboratories @ NTU, I saw how much of chip verification still depends on slow, manual inspection, and how little labelled data exists to automate it. I am driven by problems like this: the stakes are real and the solution does not yet exist. While building those pipelines, I kept running into questions that engineering alone cannot answer. One example is how a model can learn reliably from a handful of noisy, real-world examples. A PhD would give me the time, rigour and mentorship to answer those questions. It would also let me move from applying AI to hardware problems to creating new AI methods that others can build on, and to publish them where the AI community will test them, at venues such as ICLR, NeurIPS and ICML.
Past Research Experiences
Since September 2025, I have been a Project Officer in Artificial Intelligence and Computer Vision at Temasek Laboratories @ NTU, working on hardware assurance projects funded by DSO National Laboratories. The aim of this work is to verify that a chip has not been tampered with during manufacture or distribution, a step that is still largely done manually. My first project, with Assoc Prof Gwee Bah Hwee, addressed FPGA IP identification: recognizing which functional blocks are present in a chip's netlist. I built and evaluated more than ten graph neural network pipelines in PyTorch Geometric, including residual GNNs, GIN and Graph Transformers, and improved the F1 score by 7.65% on noisy netlist data. I additionally explored the application of asymmetric contrastive learning for one-class node classification, demonstrating initiative in experimenting with emerging techniques. Because a deployed model will inevitably meet circuits it was never trained on, I added energy-based out-of-distribution detection (OOD), which improved localization precision by 31.66% in relative terms while keeping accuracy above 90%. I presented this work as first author at the IEEE International Symposium on the Physical and Failure Analysis of Integrated Circuits (IPFA 2026).
My second project, in collaboration with the hardware security researcher Andrew "bunnie" Huang, used his Infra-Red, In-Situ (IRIS) imaging technique, which allows a chip's circuitry to be inspected without destroying it. I built a reproducible pipeline for classifying ASIC standard cells from IRIS images, curating a dataset of over 240,000 cell images across three classes and benchmarking eight CNN architectures, including EfficientNet, ResNet50, VGG and U-Net. The dataset and baselines are publicly available, and I presented this work as first author at the IEEE International Conference on Artificial Intelligence Circuits and Systems (AICAS 2026). Both projects taught me the same lesson. In hardware, real data is scarce, expensive to label and noisy, while clean synthetic data does not look like what analysts actually face. That gap is the starting point of my PhD proposal.
My interest in graph learning began with my Final Year Project under Prof Jagath C. Rajapakse at NTU's Biomedical Informatics Lab. I implemented GAT and GCN models to embed over 40,000 peptide–protein complexes, and built a latent diffusion model whose U-Net denoiser was conditioned on binding-site embeddings, improving on existing methods across six generative metrics. This work was published in Computers in Biology and Medicine, where I am second author. Molecules and circuits turned out to be closely related problems: both are graphs governed by strict rules, where a single changed node can change the whole function.

Current Research Interest
The AI area I want to pursue is resource-efficient AI, and specifically how to learn transferable representations of structured data when real labelled data is scarce. My proposal sits squarely within AISG's Theme Three, Resource-Efficient AI, and stays on that single theme: it contributes to learning from small data sets, AI understanding of design and creation, and knowledge representation with complex priors, rather than spreading across several themes.
Hardware is built by a chain of deterministic transformations: RTL is synthesized into gate-level netlists, then into physical layouts. Security analysis runs this chain backwards. An analyst starts from a netlist extracted from images of a chip and must recover what it does and whether it has been tampered with. What intrigues me is an unusual asymmetry. Real extracted data is rare and noisy, yet the forward tools that created it, such as logic synthesis and formal equivalence checkers, are freely available and can generate unlimited aligned examples, each with a guarantee of functional equivalence. Current AI methods barely use this structure, and I want to build methods that do. As our future becomes ever more chip oriented, I find few problems more worth solving.
My research first learns a shared representation of RTL and gate-level netlists through contrastive learning, with training pairs produced by synthesis tools and confirmed by a formal equivalence checker rather than labelled by people. It then pretrains the encoder to reconstruct masked sub-circuits, using Boolean consistency as a training signal. Next, it adapts these representations to real, noisy netlists extracted from SEM images, using a calibrated model of extraction errors and very few labels. Finally, it tests whether the framework transfers to source code and firmware binaries or can ground an LLM agent that annotates netlists. The broader aim is to show that symbolic tools can stand in for large, labelled datasets, a question that extends to program analysis, chemistry and any domain where a simulator can generate supervision but real observations are scarce. I plan four papers targeting ICLR, NeurIPS, ICML and AAAI, IJCAI or KDD, along with an open multi-level circuit benchmark that includes noisy extracted netlists.
I hope to be supervised by Assoc Prof Gwee Bah Hwee, with Assoc Prof Wen Bihan as co-supervisor. Prof Gwee's group has developed the machine learning work directly upstream of my proposal, including GraphClusNet for partitioning recovered netlists, MLConnect for correcting errors in recovered circuits, and GoCLIP for IP circuit identification. MLConnect and his group's error-aware annotation methods characterize exactly the extraction errors my third objective models, and his group has the SEM imaging and netlist-extraction capability to supply real data that most machine learning groups lack. Our joint IPFA 2026 paper is the project's starting baseline, so I would begin from working infrastructure and an established collaboration. Prof Wen complements this with expertise in self-supervised learning from noisy measurements and a strong publication record at ICML and NeurIPS, which will help me frame each contribution for AI reviewers. The two professors have already published together on anomaly detection in microscopy images and X-ray CT analysis for PCB verification.
Future Career Goals
My goal is to lead research teams building trustworthy AI for hardware security in Singapore, and the AISG Fellowship provides the vital first step. The work should be rigorous enough to publish at top AI venues and practical enough to be used by the analysts who verify the chips our infrastructure depends on. Over the next decade, I hope to grow from contributing to projects to leading them, while continuing to mentor students who, like me, arrive with a hunger for problem solving.
This research fellowship is directly aligned with that goal. The PhD would give me depth in the machine learning foundations my current work rests on, namely representation learning, self-supervision and domain adaptation, and let me produce methods and open benchmarks the hardware security community can build on. As semiconductors become central to Singapore's economy and security, the country will need people who understand both the AI and the hardware. The AISG PhD would train me to be one of them, and to stay here to do that work. I would also welcome the chance to contribute to AISG's other pillars, whether through its programme initiatives, mentoring on AI apprenticeship and learning programmes, or working with industry partners on hardware assurance problems.
